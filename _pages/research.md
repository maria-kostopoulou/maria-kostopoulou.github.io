<li><strong>Valuing a Reduction in the Risk of Non-Fatal Cancer</strong> <em>(under review, Environmental and Resource Economics)</em> with D. Herrera-Araujo, H. Andersson, D. Dussaux & O. Byström. OECD Environment. Working Papers, 2026. <a href="https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/valuing-a-reduction-in-the-risk-of-non-fatal-cancer_0345660e-en.html" target="_blank">[link]</a>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Cancer is associated with various environmental contaminants and chemicals, including heavy metals, pesticides, as well as foodborne and waterborne pathogens. Many of these hazards are subject to regulation, or may be considered for regulation, to reduce exposure and prevent human health risks. In addition to the well-known significant mortality risks, cancer also causes substantial morbidity, including debilitating symptoms and treatment side effects that severely impact patients' quality of life. Despite this, there has been no comprehensive study of the morbidity of cancer, making it difficult to fully assess the broader burden and economic implications of cancer. This paper is part of the series of large-scale willingness-to-pay (WTP) studies resulting from the Surveys on Willingness-to-pay to Avoid Negative Chemicals-Related Health Effects (SWACHE) project that intends to improve the basis for doing cost benefit analyses of chemicals management options and environmental policies in general. The present paper details a stated preference survey estimating the value of avoiding a case of non-fatal cancer (VNF). The SWACHE non-fatal cancer survey was fielded in 10 countries: Canada, Denmark, Estonia, Japan, Mexico, Norway, Slovenia, Spain, the United Kingdom and the United States. In each country, a sample of approximately 1 200 individuals representative of the general population was collected and empirically analysed. Based on this survey, the mean VNF is USD2023 Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) 883 000. The VNF ranges from USD2023 PPP 559 000 in Mexico to USD2023 PPP 1 266 000 in the United States.</p></details></li>

<li><strong>Equivalent Income Weights as a Bridge between Benefit–Cost Analysis and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis: Evidence from Six Countries.</strong> Working paper, single-authored.
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Benefit–cost analysis and cost-effectiveness analysis add up euros of willingness to pay and quality-adjusted life years without asking who receives them. Their distributional versions correct for income in the first case and for health in the second. Equivalent income combines income and health in a single monetary measure of well-being. It is the income that, in perfect health, would leave a person as well off as they are with their actual income and health. I show that equivalent income weights coincide with both traditions under one condition on preferences, namely that the share of income individuals would give up for perfect health depends on their health but not on their income. The weights then split into the income-only weights of weighted benefit–cost analysis and a health weight that is the same at every income. With unit inequality aversion, they value money as weighted benefit–cost analysis does and health as cost-effectiveness analysis does. I test this condition with a double-bounded contingent valuation survey of 9,428 respondents in six European countries, in which illness profiles are randomly assigned. The condition does not hold in any of the specifications I estimate. The poor would sacrifice a larger share of their income for perfect health than the rich, and a severe illness costs them a larger proportion of their equivalent income. With unit inequality aversion, equivalent income weights still value money as weighted benefit–cost analysis does, but they give more weight to a health improvement among the poor than among the rich, whereas cost-effectiveness analysis counts both equally. With greater inequality aversion, they also decline more steeply with income than income-only weights and favour the severely ill, especially among the poor. For policy appraisal, equivalent income weights offer a single criterion for money and health, built from people's own trade-offs rather than from an assumed utility function, which shows when the two traditions are adequate and how to correct them when they are not.</p></details></li>

<li><strong>Willingness-to-Pay for Environmental Health: A QALY-Based Study Across Europe</strong> with D. Herrera-Araujo, S. Navrud, H. Lindhjem, G. Appéré & M. Travers. Working paper.
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>We estimate individuals' willingness to pay (WTP) for environmental health improvements using a harmonized survey across six European countries (France, Italy, Spain, Greece, Poland, and Sweden). The study adopts a Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY) framework and presents hypothetical illness episodes that vary in severity and duration, allowing us to identify how respondents trade off short, severe conditions against longer, milder ones. To compare prevention and treatment valuations, respondents are randomly assigned to a probability module or a certainty module. The probability module employs a discrete choice experiment to elicit WTP for reductions in fatal and non-fatal health risks, while the certainty module uses a double-bounded dichotomous choice design to elicit WTP for faster recovery from an existing illness; respondents failing probability training are reassigned to the certainty module. We further test for disease-specific premiums by randomly labeling health states. The estimates provide policy-relevant values for morbidity and mortality in environmental cost-benefit analysis across Europe.</p></details></li>

<li><strong>Valuing Multiple Health Risk Reductions from Air Pollution and Chemical Exposure: Can Aggregation Bias Be Ignored?</strong> with M. Travers, G. Appéré, S. Navrud, H. Lindhjem & D. Herrera-Araujo. Working paper.
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Policies that reduce air pollution and chemical exposure often generate several health benefits simultaneously. Economic evaluations, however, frequently aggregate WTP estimates elicited separately for morbidity-risk reductions. This paper examines whether this potential aggregation bias can be ignored when preferences over multiple health risks are non-additive. We develop an analytical framework that extends the standard two-risk case to any number of morbidity risks and decompose WTP aggregation bias into two mechanisms. Independent Valuation and Summation (IVS) bias arises from cross-risk interactions when each risk reduction is valued against a common multi-risk baseline. Separate Valuation and Summation (SVS) bias arises when each risk is valued in isolation and respondents ignore, downplay, or misperceive non-valued risks. We assess these mechanisms using a Pseudo-Separate Valuation procedure embedded in a discrete choice experiment on asthma, chronic kidney disease, and type 2 diabetes in 6 European countries. IVS bias is small for marginal risk changes, whereas SVS bias is large and sensitive to assumptions about perceived background risks. Accounting for these mechanisms is therefore important for the economic appraisal of environmental health policies and benefit transfer. Aggregating health-risk values is not a mechanical summation problem, but requires explicit assumptions about risk interactions and perceived non-valued risks.</p></details></li>

<li><strong>Monetary Valuation of Pollution-Related Morbidity: A Review of Evidence from 2006–2023</strong> with S. Åström, A. Eriksson, S. Schucht, G. Appéré, M. Travers, et al. Working paper.
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This study reviews the economic valuations of morbidity, related to air and chemical pollution, addressing a data gap in current socio-economic impact assessments used to support environmental and health policies. We analysed literature from 2006 to 2023, covering 22 relevant health effects. The review process initially identified 10,243 studies, of which 298 unique studies were included. From these, 540 values were extracted. The studies were categorized based on several attributes, including the definition of the health effect, country of focus, study methodology, cost approach (incidence or prevalence), cost categories (direct, indirect, and disutility), and cost components. We standardized the results to annual timeframes where possible, and adjusted values to €2020 for consistency. Premature birth, dementia, lung cancer, and hypertension were found to have the highest economic values. Heart failure emerged as the condition with the most available studies, particularly regarding direct medical expenditures. The review highlighted a lack of economic valuations for reproductive system disorders, signalling an area for future research. The findings offer valuable insights into the economic costs of various conditions, uncovering significant patterns and identifying sufficiently robust valuations that can complement existing pollution policy cost-benefit analysis (CBA) models, such as CaRBonH and BenMAP. However, considerable variation exists both within and between health effects and cost categories, primarily due to the diverse methodologies used across studies. This review underscores the urgent need for standardized approaches in environmental health economics to inform and strengthen policy decisions.</p></details></li>

<li><strong>The Hidden Cost of Heatwaves: Obstetric Outcomes and Hospital Congestion in France.</strong> Single-authored.
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Heatwaves affect health not only through direct physiological exposure, but also by placing pressure on healthcare systems when sharp increases in demand coincide with limited hospital capacity. This paper studies how heatwave-related congestion affects obstetric care in France. The analysis uses individual-level administrative data from the Système National des Données de Santé covering all deliveries in France between 2003 and 2019, linked to temperature records and emergency-department indicators of healthcare system pressure. The empirical strategy exploits within-heatwave variation by comparing women in their ninth month of pregnancy who deliver at different points within a heatwave event, when accumulated demand is greatest and working conditions for clinical staff are likely to be most strained. This approach helps separate the effects of healthcare system congestion from direct heat exposure. By following mothers beyond delivery, the paper estimates both the immediate and longer-term morbidity. The results provide the first evidence for France on the indirect health costs of heatwave-related healthcare congestion in obstetric care.</p></details></li>
